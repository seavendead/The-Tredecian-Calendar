# The-Tredecian-Calendar
A sensible calendar that solves for equivalency of all months, includes all zodiac signs, better aligns with our moon cycle, and natural rythm, while using names that actually make sense and sound beautiful. 

Tredecian (treh-DEE-shun), from the Latin tredecim, "thirteen," plus -ian, "of." Like the Julian and Gregorian calendars, the name simply means "the calendar of thirteen," and said aloud it carries the rhythm of the word tradition which is quite nice.

Overview

The Tredecian Calendar is a perpetual calendar of 13 identical months of 28 days.

Every month is exactly four weeks, starting on a Sunday and ending on a Saturday.
Every date falls on the same weekday every year, so the calendar never changes.
The year begins on Monia 1 (Gregorian March 1), where the ancient Roman year began. That start is what lets every month name match its number.
13 × 28 = 364 days. The remaining day, 'Year Day', closes the year (Gregorian Feb 28). In leap years, every four years, 'Leap Day' follows it (Gregorian Feb 29). Both sit outside the week, so the weekly cycle never slips. These are essentially days of break.

Each month is a number root plus the ending -ia. The roots are Greek, except bi and non, which are borrowed from Latin because they are easier to say. In months 11 to 13, the "d" stands for ten, the way eleven, twelve and thirteen grow out of ten.

Over a year, the sun passes through thirteen constellations: the twelve familiar zodiac signs plus Ophiuchus (oh-fee-YOO-kus), the serpent bearer, between Scorpio and Sagittarius. Because the real constellations are uneven in size, the calendar divides the sun's yearly path into 13 equal signs, one per month. Like the traditional zodiac, the signs follow the seasons rather than the slowly drifting stars, opening with Aries and closing with Pisces.

Why it is simpler
One month layout to learn, instead of months of 28, 29, 30 and 31 days.

Every month has the same days, workdays and weekends, so months compare cleanly. Payday would always be the same.

Holidays land on the same weekday every year, and floating holidays like Thanksgiving become fixed dates.
